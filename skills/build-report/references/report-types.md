# Report types

Use this file at checkpoint 1 (Intake) once the user has picked a report type, and again at checkpoint 3 (Outline) to shape the page list. Each type sets the audience, length, tone, page structure and how much evidence to carry. Layout and styling follow the Quallie server's report design rules; this file covers only what goes into the report.

Evidence density means how many quotes or clips back each finding. Quotes and clips must come from the project's own data, never invented.

## Executive summary

- **Audience:** senior stakeholders who will read for a few minutes and act on it.
- **When it is used:** at the end of a study, or alongside a full debrief for people who will not read the whole thing.
- **Length:** 1 to 3 pages.
- **Tone:** direct and confident. Lead with the answer, not the journey.
- **Page structure:**
  1. The decision or answer the study supports, stated in one or two sentences.
  2. About 3 key findings, each with its one strongest piece of evidence.
  3. Recommendations: what to do next, tied to the findings.
  4. Method in one line (who, how many, how).
- **Evidence density:** one clip per finding. Choose the clip a sceptical executive would find most convincing.
- **Leave out:** detailed method, sample breakdowns, minor themes, caveats that do not change the decision, long quote walls.
- **Question to ask:** "What decision is this summary meant to inform?"

## Progress report (external)

- **Audience:** the client, mid-fieldwork.
- **When it is used:** partway through fieldwork, to show momentum and flag what is surfacing.
- **Length:** 1 to 2 pages.
- **Tone:** measured and transparent. Label every theme as early or emerging.
- **Page structure:**
  1. What is done: sessions completed, methods used.
  2. Sample so far, and any gaps against the plan.
  3. Emerging themes, each labelled early or emerging.
  4. What is next: remaining fieldwork and when the full findings land.
- **Evidence density:** at most one quote per emerging theme, enough to make it concrete.
- **Leave out:** firm conclusions and recommendations. It is too early, and a client may act on them.
- **Question to ask:** "Is there anything the client is waiting to hear about, so we can say where it stands?"

## Working session (internal)

- **Audience:** the research team, for brainstorming and synthesis.
- **When it is used:** during or right after fieldwork, before anyone has settled on the story.
- **Length:** whatever the material needs, usually 1 to 3 pages.
- **Tone:** deliberately rough and not polished. It is a thinking surface, not a deliverable.
- **Page structure:**
  1. Provocations: bold statements the data might support, to argue with.
  2. Tensions: places where the data pulls in two directions.
  3. Contradictory quotes side by side.
  4. Open questions the team has not answered yet.
  5. "What if" prompts to push the thinking further.
- **Evidence density:** high and raw. Several quotes per tension, deliberately including the ones that disagree.
- **Leave out:** recommendations, executive framing, and polish that makes ideas look settled.
- **Question to ask:** "Which question is the team most stuck on right now?"

## Full debrief

- **Audience:** the client team, including people who will work with the findings day to day.
- **When it is used:** at the end of the study, as the main deliverable.
- **Length:** several pages.
- **Tone:** thorough and evidenced, but led by insight rather than by method.
- **Page structure:**
  1. Context and objectives: why the study was run and what it set out to learn.
  2. Method and sample: who took part, how, and how many.
  3. Themes with evidence: a page per major theme, or per group of related themes.
  4. Segments or personas, only if the data supports them.
  5. Implications and recommendations.
  6. Appendix, optional: discussion guide, extra detail, full sample breakdown.
- **Evidence density:** two or three quotes or clips per theme, chosen to show range, not repetition.
- **Leave out:** segments or personas the data cannot support, and themes resting on a single participant unless flagged as such.
- **Question to ask:** "Who on the client team will use this most, and what do they need to take away?"

## Topline

- **Audience:** the client or the internal team, right after fieldwork.
- **When it is used:** as a fast first read before full analysis is done.
- **Length:** one page.
- **Tone:** clearly provisional. Say up front that the full analysis may refine these findings.
- **Page structure:**
  1. A provisional label and the date fieldwork closed.
  2. Headline findings only, 3 to 5 of them, each a bullet's worth of text.
  3. One supporting quote for each headline finding.
- **Evidence density:** one quote per finding.
- **Leave out:** recommendations, method detail beyond one line, segments, secondary themes.
- **Question to ask:** "When is the full report due, so the topline can say so?"

## Other

When none of the five types fits:

1. Find out three things, one question per message as SKILL.md requires: who the audience is, what the report is for, and roughly how long it should be. Skip any the user already answered at intake.
2. Map the answers to the closest type above, and tell the user which one you chose and why.
3. Use that type's structure as the starting point, adjusted to what the user told you.

- **Question to ask first:** "Who will read this, and what should they do after reading it?"

## Choosing between close types

- Executive summary or topline: a topline is provisional and comes right after fieldwork; an executive summary is final and carries recommendations.
- Progress report or working session: a progress report goes to the client and stays cautious; a working session stays inside the team and can be provocative.
- Full debrief or executive summary: if the user has time for only one, ask who the main reader is. Senior readers get the executive summary; working teams get the full debrief.
